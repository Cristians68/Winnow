What the reviews actually say.

Winnow analyses the reviews on an Amazon product page and shows you the rating that product would have if its manipulated reviews were removed — along with exactly why.

Add to Chrome — free See how it works

Works in Chrome, Edge, Brave and Opera. No account, no tracking, no affiliate links. Read the source — the scoring engine is open so you can check the grade rather than trust it.

Why this exists

Mozilla shut Fakespot down in July 2025, and ReviewMeta went dark in early 2026. Ten million people lost the tools they used to sanity-check reviews, and nothing credible replaced them.

Meanwhile the problem got worse. AI-generated fake reviews nearly doubled as a share of fake reviews in a single year, and 74% of them are five stars — against 59% of genuine reviews.

The replacements have their own problems. The most-recommended Fakespot alternative ships an undisclosed Amazon affiliate tag on its "Buy Now" button. And Honey lost roughly eight million users after being caught overwriting creators' affiliate links. A tool that earns a commission when you buy cannot credibly tell you not to buy.

How it works

Winnow scores every review it can see on the page, then recomputes the product's rating with the suspicious ones down-weighted. Nothing is hidden behind a number — every signal shows its reasoning.

Rating distribution

Genuine products keep a one-star tail from damage and defects. Campaigns can add five-star reviews but cannot remove organic one-star ones, so a perfect spread with no negative tail is a tell.

Review language

Disclosed incentives, template-farm boilerplate, and the uniform rhythm and missing specifics that mark generated text.

Duplicate text

Review farms paraphrase from templates. Character-level comparison catches what word matching misses.

Review timing

Paid campaigns land in waves. Organic reviews trickle in with sales.

Substance and response

Five-star ratings carrying six generic words. Featured reviews months old that nobody ever found helpful.

Cross-product patterns

Optional deeper analysis spots phrasing reused across unrelated products, reviewer accounts that move together, and listings that swapped product while keeping their reviews.

How we make money

Sponsorship, in Winnow's own windows only.

One sponsored message appears in Winnow's toolbar popup and settings page. It never appears on an Amazon page and never appears beside a grade. You can switch it off in Settings, and nothing else changes when you do.

A sponsor is told that a Winnow window opened, and nothing else. The request carries no product, no page address, no search term, no grade and no identifier — there is no field in it that could hold one. So a sponsor cannot buy placement against a particular product, because it cannot know which one you are looking at.

Still true, still binding in our privacy policy: no affiliate links, no referral tags, no merchant relationships, and no payment from any seller, brand or marketplace to influence a grade.

The scoring engine is open source specifically so you can verify that no merchant is paying for a better grade. You don't have to take our word for it, which is the point. A tool that earns a commission when you buy cannot credibly tell you not to buy — which is exactly why the money comes from a slot that cannot see what you are buying.

What leaves your browser

Everything happens on your device. The extension you can install makes no network requests at all — it holds no permission to contact any server, which you can check in its manifest.

A future optional feature would send data about a product listing — never about you, and only when you click a button to ask for it. It is not part of the current release, and turning it on would require a new permission you would see and approve first. The table below describes that feature's limits, so they are on the record before it exists.

What Winnow sends and never sends
Sent, only on requestNever sent
The product's public Amazon ID, its rating, and the reviews already shown on the page Any identifier for you — no account, user ID, device ID or cookie
A hash of the product title, never the title Your Amazon session, orders, addresses or payment details
Amazon's public reviewer IDs, hashed before storage Your browsing history or any page you didn't ask about

The server rejects any request carrying a field it doesn't expect, rather than ignoring it. If a future version were changed to send something extra, the request would fail rather than quietly succeed.

Winnow also never crawls Amazon using your session. Automated scraping through a logged-in session can get your Amazon account suspended, so Winnow reads only what your browser already loaded. That costs some depth, and it's a trade we make on purpose.

What Winnow doesn't claim

Every result is an estimate, not proof. Winnow cannot know that any individual review is fake — it identifies patterns that manipulated reviews tend to produce, and it tells you what each judgement rested on.

Usually only 8–13 reviews are visible on a product page, and those are featured reviews — a biased sample. Winnow shrinks its adjustment to account for that, and declines to state an adjusted rating at all when too little of the sample looks trustworthy. "Couldn't read this page" is never a verdict about the product.

Questions

Why an extension instead of a website?
Because a website can't do this any more. Since May 2026, Amazon returns "Page Not Found" to any server requesting review pages without session cookies, and full review text left the public HTML. Every tool that analyses from a server is now working blind — which is why they all return roughly the same score for everything. An extension reads the page you're already looking at.
Does it slow down Amazon?
No. The grade is computed from the page your browser already rendered, in about a millisecond, with no extra requests.
Will this get my Amazon account banned?
No. Winnow makes no automated requests to Amazon at all. It only reads what your browser already loaded for a page you chose to visit.
What if it says a good product is bad?
Open the breakdown and check the reasoning — every flag shows why it fired. If it looks wrong, it may well be. Amazon changes its page markup often, and a parsing failure can produce a bad grade. Winnow tries to detect that and say so, and we'd rather hear about it: report it on GitHub.
Which sites does it work on?
Amazon storefronts only, in fifteen countries. Doing one marketplace properly beats doing several badly, which is the failing of every current alternative.

Check a listing before you buy it

Winnow reads the reviews already on the page and shows the rating the product would have without the manipulated ones. It never uploads the page, and it never sends you anywhere to buy anything.
