Looking for a Fakespot alternative?

Mozilla shut Fakespot down on 1 July 2025 and did not replace it. Winnow does the same job a different way: it reads the reviews already on the Amazon page you are looking at, and shows the rating that product would have without the ones that look manipulated.

What actually happened to Fakespot

Mozilla acquired Fakespot in 2023. On its own blog it announced that the Fakespot extensions, apps and website closed on 1 July 2025, and that the Firefox Review Checker feature closed on 10 June 2025. The reason Mozilla gave was that "while the idea resonated, it didn't fit a model we could sustain".

No replacement shipped. The announcement pointed readers toward unrelated Firefox features — tab groups and bookmarks. ReviewMeta, the other tool people relied on, went dark separately. Millions of people who had a habit of checking a listing before buying were left with nothing to check it with.

That gap is the only reason Winnow exists. It is not a fork of Fakespot and shares no code with it; it is an independent tool built after the shutdown, for the same job.

How Winnow differs from how Fakespot worked

Fakespot analysed listings on its own servers. That model stopped being viable for a second reason beyond Mozilla's: since May 2026, Amazon's /product-reviews/ pages return 404 without session cookies, and full review bodies left the public HTML. A server that crawls Amazon from the outside can no longer see the reviews it needs.

Winnow reads the page your browser already rendered. Nothing is crawled and nothing is uploaded, which is why the analysis happens on your device rather than in a data centre. It also means Winnow will never make automated requests to Amazon using your session — that would put your own Amazon account at risk of suspension, and no future version will do it.

What you actually get

  • An adjusted rating. The star average the product would have without the reviews that failed Winnow's checks — or an honest refusal, when too little of the sample is trustworthy to say.
  • The reasoning, not just a score. Seven checks, each shown with what it found and whether it changed the grade. A verdict you cannot inspect is a verdict you have to take on faith.
  • Its own limits, stated. Amazon usually shows only 8–13 reviews on a product page, and those are featured reviews — a biased sample. Winnow shrinks its adjustment to account for that and says so on screen.

What it deliberately does not do

  • No affiliate links, ever. No referral tags, no "buy now" button carrying a commission. A tool that earns money when you buy cannot credibly tell you not to buy. This is written into the privacy policy as a binding commitment rather than a current policy.
  • No claim about the product itself. Winnow measures review integrity. It never reads whether the reviews say the product is good, and it never tells you whether to buy.
  • No account, no sign-in, no tracking. There is nothing to register for.

Check the permissions yourself

Every Chrome extension has to declare, on its own store listing, what data it handles. Google shows that declaration under Privacy practices, and the developer is accountable for it. It is the fastest way to judge any review-checking tool, including this one.

Winnow declares one permission: storage — used to keep your settings and the grades you have already seen, on your device. It asks for access to Amazon storefronts and no other site. You can read the exact manifest in the public repository before installing.

Open the store listing of any alternative you are considering, scroll to Privacy practices, and compare. If a review-integrity tool collects more about you than it needs to read a page you are already on, that is worth knowing before you install it — whichever tool you end up choosing.
