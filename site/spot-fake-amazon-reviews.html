How to spot fake Amazon reviews

Seven checks you can do by eye, in about a minute, without installing anything. They are the same checks Winnow shows inside its own panel, because a tool that only works while you are running it has taught you nothing.

1. Read the 1- and 2-star reviews first

This is the single highest-value habit on this page. Negative reviews are the hardest to fake — there is no market for paying people to complain — and they tell you what actually goes wrong with the thing. A product with a 4.6 average and a consistent complaint about the hinge breaking at six months is a product with a hinge problem, whatever the average says.

2. Watch for reviews describing a different product

A phone case reviewed on a listing for a desk lamp means the listing was changed and the old rating came along with it. This is one of the most common forms of review manipulation on Amazon and one of the easiest to see: the seller keeps the star average an unrelated product earned and swaps in new inventory underneath it.

A related tell is a rating split with nothing in the middle — lots of five stars, a visible tail of one stars, and almost no threes. Genuine products collect a middle.

3. Check who you are actually buying from

Look just under the Buy box. "Ships from and sold by Amazon" is a different proposition from a seller name you have never seen, and returns work differently. This costs two seconds and is not about reviews at all, which is exactly why it gets skipped.

4. Trust reviews with customer photos more

Photographs are far more work to fake than a paragraph of text, and photos taken in a recognisably ordinary home more so. They are not proof, but they move the odds considerably.

5. Reverse image search the product photos

If the same item appears under several unfamiliar brand names, you are usually looking at a generic product being resold rather than something a company designed and stands behind. That is not automatically bad — plenty of good products are made this way — but it tells you the brand name carries no information, so the reviews have to carry all of it.

6. Be wary of a new listing that already has hundreds of five-star ratings

Genuine reviews accumulate at roughly the speed of sales. A listing that is a few weeks old with four hundred ratings and a 4.9 average did not get there the slow way.

7. Check what your shopping extensions can reach

Worth doing once, for everything you have installed. Open chrome://extensions (or about:addons in Firefox), find each extension, and read its permissions. A coupon or review tool that can read every site you visit is a tool that is reading every site you visit.

Apply this to Winnow too. It asks for one permission — storage — plus access to Amazon storefronts, and nothing else. It cannot read your passwords, your logins, or any other site. You can check that in the public source before installing, and on the store listing after.

What none of this can tell you

No method on this page, and no tool including Winnow, can tell you that a particular review is fake. What is actually being measured is patterns — text that repeats across reviews, ratings arriving in bursts, a missing negative tail — and patterns give you odds, not proof.

Be suspicious of any tool that gives you a confident score for everything you point it at. A checker that returns roughly the same reassuring number for every listing is not detecting anything; it is decorating the page. The useful signal is a tool that sometimes says I could not tell.

It is also worth separating two different questions. "Are these reviews trustworthy?" and "is this product good?" are not the same question, and a tool that answers the first should not pretend to answer the second.
